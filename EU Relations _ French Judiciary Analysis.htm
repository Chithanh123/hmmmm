Relationship with European Regional International Courts

This section examines the complex relationship between the French judiciary and European regional courts, focusing on the integration of EU law, the preliminary reference procedure, and the implementation of European court decisions.

Monist Theory of International Law

Constitutional Foundation

France adheres to a monist approach to international law, as established by Article 55 of the Constitution:

"Treaties or agreements duly ratified or approved shall, upon publication, prevail over Acts of Parliament, subject, with respect to each agreement or treaty, to its application by the other party."

This provision establishes a clear hierarchy of norms in which international treaties, including EU treaties, have authority superior to domestic legislation. However, they remain subordinate to the Constitution itself, as confirmed by the Constitutional Council in its Decision No. 2004-505 DC of November 19, 2004, regarding the Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe.

EU Law Integration

The integration of European Union law into the French legal system is specifically addressed by Article 88-1 of the Constitution, added in 1992 following the Maastricht Treaty:

"The Republic shall participate in the European Union constituted by States which have freely chosen to exercise some of their powers in common by virtue of the Treaty on European Union and of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, as they result from the treaty signed in Lisbon on 13 December, 2007."

This article provides a specific constitutional foundation for France's participation in the EU and the resulting obligations, including the implementation of EU law and compliance with CJEU decisions.

Judicial Recognition

The monist approach has been progressively recognized and implemented by French courts:

  • Court of Cassation: In the landmark Café Jacques Vabre decision (May 24, 1975), the Court of Cassation recognized the primacy of European Community law over subsequent national legislation based on Article 55 of the Constitution.
  • Council of State: Initially reluctant, the Council of State eventually accepted the primacy of EU law in the Nicolo decision (October 20, 1989), allowing administrative courts to set aside domestic legislation incompatible with EU treaties.

These judicial developments have been crucial in ensuring the effective implementation of EU law in France, despite the absence of an explicit supremacy clause in the French Constitution.

Limitations of the Monist Approach

Despite the constitutional recognition of international law primacy, certain limitations exist:

  • Constitutional identity reservation: The Constitutional Council has established that the obligation to transpose EU directives does not override rules or principles inherent to France's constitutional identity (Decision No. 2006-540 DC of July 27, 2006).
  • Reciprocity condition: Article 55 subjects the primacy of treaties to their application by the other party, though the CJEU has rejected this condition for EU law in Commission v. France (Case 232/78).
  • Constitutional review: International treaties, including EU treaties, can be subject to constitutional review before ratification, potentially requiring constitutional amendments if incompatibilities are found.

These limitations reflect the ongoing tension between national sovereignty and European integration in the French legal system.

Key EU Treaty Provisions
  • Article 267 TFEU (Preliminary Reference Procedure)

    "The Court of Justice of the European Union shall have jurisdiction to give preliminary rulings concerning: (a) the interpretation of the Treaties; (b) the validity and interpretation of acts of the institutions, bodies, offices or agencies of the Union..."

  • Article 258 TFEU (Infringement Procedure)

    "If the Commission considers that a Member State has failed to fulfil an obligation under the Treaties, it shall deliver a reasoned opinion on the matter after giving the State concerned the opportunity to submit its observations. If the State concerned does not comply with the opinion within the period laid down by the Commission, the latter may bring the matter before the Court of Justice of the European Union."

  • Article 4(3) TEU (Principle of Sincere Cooperation)

    "Pursuant to the principle of sincere cooperation, the Union and the Member States shall, in full mutual respect, assist each other in carrying out tasks which flow from the Treaties."

  • Article 19(1) TEU (Effective Judicial Protection)

    "Member States shall provide remedies sufficient to ensure effective legal protection in the fields covered by Union law."

Landmark European Court Decisions

Court of Justice of the EU

  • Case 6/64 Costa v ENEL (1964): Established the primacy of EU law over national law
  • Case 106/77 Simmenthal (1978): National courts must set aside national legislation incompatible with EU law
  • Joined Cases C-188/10 and C-189/10 Melki (2010): Addressed the compatibility of the French QPC procedure with EU law
  • Case C-416/17 Commission v France (2018): Found France in breach of the Nitrates Directive

European Court of Human Rights

  • Kruslin v. France (1990): Telephone tapping legislation violated Article 8 ECHR
  • Selmouni v. France (1999): Police mistreatment constituted torture under Article 3 ECHR
  • Moulin v. France (2010): French prosecutors lack independence to qualify as "judicial authority"
  • S.A.S. v. France (2014): Ban on face coverings in public spaces did not violate ECHR
